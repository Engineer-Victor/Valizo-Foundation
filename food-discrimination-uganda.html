Food Discrimination Against Children in Uganda

The Role of Uganda’s Nutrient Profile Model Towards Protecting Children’s Nutritional Rights.

Introduction

This article by Luleti David and Obusinge Peace Flavia examines food discrimination against children in Uganda and the role of the Nutrient Profile Model (NPM) in protecting children’s nutritional rights. It raises concerns about differences in food quality, affordability and the availability of nutritious products, and considers how these issues relate to public health and the right to adequate food.

The authors describe nutrient profiling as the science of classifying or ranking foods according to their nutritional composition for purposes related to disease prevention and health promotion. They identify the Nutrient Profile Model as a tool that can guide policy on front-of-pack warning labels, food marketing, taxation and consumer education.

The article calls for an evidence-based Nutrient Profile Model to strengthen food regulation, improve consumer information and help safeguard children’s nutritional rights.

Poor-Quality Food Imports and Production

The article raises concern about the importation and production of food products containing excessive levels of nutrients of critical concern, particularly sugar, salt and fats. It argues that the supply of lower-quality products, alongside the high prices of good-quality food, can deepen food inequality and make nutritious choices less accessible.

The authors link the increasing availability of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) to Uganda’s double burden of malnutrition. They explain that foods high in empty calories, sodium and free sugars can displace traditional nutrient-dense diets while contributing to overweight and obesity and worsening micronutrient deficiencies, including deficiencies in iron, zinc and vitamin A.

The article cites World Health Organization guidance that free sugars should account for less than 10% of daily energy intake, with a further reduction to below 5% preferred. It also highlights the recommended salt consumption level of less than 5 grams per day and the health risks associated with excessive salt intake.

Uganda’s Nutrition Transition

The article describes a shift in food choices from traditional diets towards ultra-processed foods, particularly among young people and children in urban centres and school settings. It discusses the possible consequences of this transition, including diet-related non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and obesity.

It further argues that poor nutrition can affect children’s health, learning and long-term productivity. The authors connect this concern to Uganda’s Human Capital Index and call for stronger implementation of food laws, policies and related public health tools.

The Role of Uganda’s Nutrient Profile Model in Safeguarding Children

The authors identify the Nutrient Profile Model as a foundation for policy measures intended to protect children and support healthier food environments. The model can help guide:

  • Front-of-pack warning labels: helping consumers identify packaged foods high in nutrients of critical concern.
  • Fiscal policies: informing measures such as taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages.
  • Marketing restrictions: supporting regulation of the promotion of unhealthy foods to children.
  • School food environments: providing a basis for food procurement guidelines and restrictions on the sale, distribution and promotion of foods exceeding nutrient thresholds in and around schools.
  • Public health planning: helping identify priority foods for fortification or supplementation.

The article also emphasises consumers’ right to information about the nutritional content of goods and services, and the importance of objective, science-based criteria for distinguishing foods according to their nutritional composition.

Corporate Negligence on Food Production

The article argues that food manufacturers owe a duty of care to consumers and should take reasonable precautions to safeguard their lives and health. It presents the Nutrient Profile Model as a way to establish thresholds for nutrients of critical concern and to support clearer warnings about high levels of sugar, salt and fats.

The authors further argue that products exceeding relevant thresholds should not be allowed to make misleading positive health or nutrition claims that could give consumers an inaccurate impression of the product.

Regulation of Aggressive Marketing of Empty Calories

The authors discuss how advertising and promotion can influence the food choices of children and young people. They recognise that freedom of expression is protected, while arguing that commercial communication may be subject to proportionate limits where it conflicts with public health and other rights.

In the article’s view, implementing the Nutrient Profile Model could support regulation of the marketing of unhealthy foods and encourage food industries to reformulate products. It frames this as a public-interest measure intended to protect health and access to adequate food.

Limitation of Deceptive Advertisements

The article raises concern about exaggerated or misleading food advertisements aimed at children and parents. It describes the use of influencers, cartoons and other child-appealing promotional techniques to encourage consumption of products that may be high in empty calories.

The authors discuss the need to balance commercial expression with the rights to health, life, adequate food and sufficient information. They refer to proportionality principles and consider measures such as sugar-sweetened beverage taxes as possible tools within a broader regulatory approach.

Challenges and Considerations

Insufficient Regulatory and Enforcement Capacity

The article identifies gaps in the specificity and enforcement of Uganda’s existing legal framework for regulating nutrient content. It argues that effective implementation of the Nutrient Profile Model would give regulatory bodies a clearer basis for setting and enforcing food standards that protect consumers, including children.

Potential Resistance from the Food Industry

The authors anticipate that manufacturers of highly processed foods, including sugary snacks and beverages, may view the Nutrient Profile Model as a threat to profit margins and brand reputation. They maintain that the model is intended to address public health concerns rather than remove lawful trading rights or consumers’ freedom of choice.

Recommendations

  1. Consumer awareness and education: conduct education campaigns on adequate food and nutrition so that children and other consumers can better understand labels and nutrition information.
  2. Monitoring and evaluation: establish mechanisms to track nutrient intake, compliance, dietary patterns and health outcomes, including stunting, obesity and non-communicable disease incidence.
  3. Equitable policies: consider affordability and rural-urban disparities, and integrate nutrition objectives into social protection and food assistance programmes so vulnerable populations can access nutrient-dense foods.
  4. Mandatory front-of-pack warning labels: adopt clear, high-contrast warning labels, such as “HIGH IN SUGAR” or “HIGH IN SODIUM”, for packaged foods exceeding Nutrient Profile Model thresholds.
  5. Product reformulation and local food system support: enforce reformulation targets for manufacturers and consider fiscal measures, including sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, to support local, fresh and indigenous nutrient-dense food systems.
