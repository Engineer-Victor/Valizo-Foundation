Food Discrimination Against Children in Uganda

The Role of Uganda’s Nutrient Profile Model Towards Protecting Children’s Nutritional Rights

By Luleti David and Obusinge Peace Flavia Research and advocacy article
Read the original PDF Download article

Introduction

The article examines food inequality and the quality of food products available to children and consumers in Uganda. It argues that differences in food quality, affordability and market practices raise important questions about the right to adequate food, public health safeguards and corporate accountability.

It introduces nutrient profiling as the science of classifying or ranking foods according to their nutritional composition, with the aim of preventing disease and promoting health. The article identifies the Nutrient Profile Model (NPM) as a tool that can guide policies on front-of-pack warning labels, food marketing, taxation and consumer education.

The authors also discuss Uganda’s legal framework on food safety, including provisions of the Food and Drugs Act and the Penal Code, and argue that an evidence-based NPM can support clearer standards for identifying foods of concern.

Central argument

The article calls for the effective implementation of Uganda’s Nutrient Profile Model to strengthen food regulation, improve consumer information and better protect children’s nutritional rights.

Poor-quality food imports and production

The authors raise concern about the importation and production of food products that contain excessive levels of nutrients of critical concern, particularly sugar, salt and fats. They argue that lower-quality products and the high price of healthier alternatives can deepen food inequality and limit access to nutritious food.

The article links the increased availability of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) to Uganda’s double burden of malnutrition. It notes that diets dominated by foods high in empty calories, sodium and free sugars can displace nutrient-dense traditional foods while contributing to overweight and obesity and worsening micronutrient deficiencies.

It refers to World Health Organization guidance that free sugars should account for less than 10% of daily energy intake, with a further reduction to below 5% preferred. The article also highlights concerns about high salt consumption and its relationship with high blood pressure, heart disease and stroke.

Uganda’s nutrition transition

The article describes a shift in food choices from traditional diets towards ultra-processed foods, particularly among young people and children in urban centres and school settings. It discusses the possible consequences of this transition, including diet-related non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and obesity.

It further argues that poor nutrition can affect children’s health, learning and long-term productivity. The authors connect this concern to Uganda’s Human Capital Index and call for stronger implementation of food laws, policies and related tools.

The role of Uganda’s Nutrient Profile Model in safeguarding children

The article identifies several policy areas that the NPM could help guide:

  • Front-of-pack warning labels: helping consumers identify packaged foods high in nutrients of critical concern.
  • Fiscal measures: informing policies such as taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages.
  • Marketing restrictions: supporting measures to regulate the promotion of unhealthy foods to children.
  • School food environments: providing a basis for food procurement guidelines and restrictions on the sale and promotion of foods that exceed nutrient thresholds in and around schools.
  • Public health planning: helping identify priority foods for fortification or supplementation.

The authors also emphasise consumers’ right to information about the nutritional content of goods and services, and the importance of objective, science-based criteria for distinguishing foods according to their nutritional composition.

Corporate negligence in food production

The article argues that food manufacturers have a duty to take reasonable care for the safety of consumers. It presents the NPM as a way to establish thresholds for nutrients of critical concern and to support clearer warnings about high levels of sugar, salt and fats.

It further proposes that products exceeding relevant thresholds should not be allowed to make misleading positive health or nutrition claims that could give consumers an inaccurate impression of the product.

Regulation of aggressive marketing of empty calories

The authors discuss how advertising and promotion can influence the food choices of children and young people. They recognise that freedom of expression is protected, while arguing that commercial communication may be subject to proportionate limits where it conflicts with public health and other rights.

In the article’s view, implementing the NPM could support regulation of the marketing of unhealthy foods and encourage food industries to reformulate products. It frames this as a public-interest measure intended to protect health and access to adequate food.

Limitation of deceptive advertisements

The article raises concern about exaggerated or misleading food advertisements aimed at children and parents. It describes the use of influencers, cartoons and other child-appealing promotional techniques to encourage consumption of products that may be high in empty calories.

It refers to legal reasoning on proportionality and argues that restrictions on misleading food advertising should balance commercial expression with the rights to health, life, adequate food and sufficient information. The authors discuss measures such as sugar-sweetened beverage taxes as possible tools within a broader regulatory approach.

Challenges and considerations

Insufficient regulatory and enforcement capacity

The article identifies gaps in the specificity and enforcement of Uganda’s existing legal framework for regulating nutrient content. It argues that effective implementation of the NPM would give regulatory bodies a clearer basis for setting and enforcing food standards that protect consumers, including children.

Potential resistance from the food industry

The authors anticipate that manufacturers of highly processed foods, including sugary snacks and beverages, may view the NPM as a threat to profit margins and brand reputation. They maintain that the model is intended to protect public health rather than remove lawful trading rights or consumers’ freedom of choice.

Recommendations

  1. Consumer awareness and education: conduct education campaigns on adequate food and nutrition so that children and other consumers can better understand labels and nutrition information.
  2. Monitoring and evaluation: establish mechanisms to track nutrient intake, compliance, dietary patterns and health outcomes, including stunting, obesity and non-communicable disease incidence.
  3. Equitable policies: consider affordability and rural-urban disparities, and integrate nutrition objectives into social protection and food assistance programmes so vulnerable populations can access nutrient-dense foods.
  4. Mandatory front-of-pack warning labels: adopt clear, high-contrast warning labels, such as “HIGH IN SUGAR” or “HIGH IN SODIUM”, for packaged foods exceeding NPM thresholds.
  5. Product reformulation and local food systems: enforce reformulation targets for manufacturers and consider fiscal measures, including sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, to support local, fresh and indigenous nutrient-dense food systems.
